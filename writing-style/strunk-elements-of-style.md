# The Elements of Style (William Strunk Jr., 1918): Rules Reference

Public-domain first edition (Ithaca, NY: privately printed, 1918; Project Gutenberg #37134). This file restates the rules as an editing reference for `byers-writing-style.md`. Where the original wording is short and well known it is quoted; otherwise the rule is paraphrased. **Note:** the full text could not be downloaded in the environment where this was compiled, so check the rules against the Gutenberg text if exact wording matters.

## II. Elementary Rules of Usage

1. **Form the possessive singular of nouns by adding *'s*.** *Charles's friend, Burns's poems.* Exceptions are ancient proper names in *-es* and *-is* (*Moses' laws*) and forms like *for conscience' sake*.
2. **In a series of three or more terms with a single conjunction, use a comma after each term except the last.** *red, white, and blue.* (The serial comma.)
3. **Enclose parenthetic expressions between commas.** Both commas, or neither. Nonrestrictive relative clauses take commas; restrictive ones do not.
4. **Place a comma before a conjunction introducing a co-ordinate clause.** *The early records of the city have disappeared, and the story of its first years can no longer be reconstructed.*
5. **Do not join independent clauses by a comma.** Use a semicolon, or a period, or a conjunction with a comma. *It is nearly half past five; we cannot reach town before dark.*
6. **Do not break sentences in two.** Don't use periods where commas belong. A dependent clause or phrase standing alone as a sentence is a fault.
7. **A participial phrase at the beginning of a sentence must refer to the grammatical subject.** Not *"Being in a dilapidated condition, I was able to buy the house very cheap,"* but *"…the house was bought very cheap."*

## III. Elementary Principles of Composition

8. **Make the paragraph the unit of composition: one paragraph to each topic.** Each new step in the argument gets its own paragraph.
9. **As a rule, begin each paragraph with a topic sentence; end it in conformity with the beginning.** The reader should know from the first sentence what the paragraph is about.
10. **Use the active voice.** "The active voice is usually more direct and vigorous than the passive." The passive is fine when the receiver of the action is the true subject.
11. **Put statements in positive form.** Make definite assertions. Avoid tame, colorless, hesitating, noncommittal language. *"He usually came late"* rather than *"He was not very often on time."* Consciously or not, the reader is dissatisfied with being told only what is not.
12. **Use definite, specific, concrete language.** Prefer the specific to the general, the definite to the vague, the concrete to the abstract. *"It rained every day for a week"* rather than *"A period of unfavorable weather set in."*
13. **Omit needless words.** "Vigorous writing is concise. A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, for the same reason that a drawing should have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts. This requires not that the writer make all his sentences short, or that he avoid all detail and treat his subjects only in outline, but that every word tell."
    - *the question as to whether* → *whether*
    - *there is no doubt but that* → *no doubt*
    - *used for fuel purposes* → *used for fuel*
    - *he is a man who* → *he*
    - *in a hasty manner* → *hastily*
    - *this is a subject which* → *this subject*
    - *the reason why is that* → *because*
    - *owing to the fact that* → *since*
    - *in spite of the fact that* → *though*
    - *call your attention to the fact that* → *remind you*
    - *the fact that he had not succeeded* → *his failure*
14. **Avoid a succession of loose sentences.** A run of two-clause sentences joined by *and, but, which, who, while* becomes monotonous. Vary the structure.
15. **Express co-ordinate ideas in similar form.** Ideas that match in content and function should match in form (parallel construction). An article or preposition that applies to every term in a series goes before the first term only, or before each term.
16. **Keep related words together.** Words that are grammatically related belong near each other. Don't separate subject from verb, or a modifier from what it modifies, with an intervening clause.
17. **In summaries, keep to one tense.** Summarize a plot or an argument in the present tense, consistently.
18. **Place the emphatic words of a sentence at the end.** The end of the sentence is usually the position of greatest prominence. The start is the second most prominent.

## IV. A Few Matters of Form (summary)

- **Headings:** leave space after the title or heading.
- **Numerals:** don't spell out dates or other serial numbers. *August 9, 1918*; *Rule 3*.
- **Parentheses:** punctuate the sentence outside the parentheses as if they were absent.
- **Quotations:** formal quotations cited as documentary evidence are introduced by a colon and enclosed in quotation marks. Quotations grammatically part of the sentence take no colon.
- **References:** in scholarly work, give references in parentheses or footnotes, not in the body of the sentence; abbreviate consistently.
- **Syllabication:** if you must break a word at the end of a line, follow the dictionary.
- **Titles:** capitalize the main words of titles of literary works; italicize them in print.

## V. Words and Expressions Commonly Misused (selection relevant to academic and essay prose)

- **All right.** Two words.
- **As good or better than.** Write *as good as or better than*, or recast.
- **As to whether.** *Whether* is enough.
- **Bid.** Past tense is *bade*; avoid in the sense "attempt" outside headlines.
- **Case.** Often unnecessary: *"In many cases, the rooms were poorly ventilated"* → *"Many of the rooms…"*
- **Certainly.** Used indiscriminately as an intensifier; avoid.
- **Character.** Often redundant: *"acts of a hostile character"* → *"hostile acts."*
- **Claim.** With an object noun, means *lay claim to*; don't use it as a substitute for *declare, maintain, charge.*
- **Compare to / compare with.** *To* points out likeness between different orders of things; *with* points out differences between things of the same order.
- **Data.** A plural, like *phenomena* and *strata*. *These data.*
- **Different than.** Write *different from*.
- **Due to.** Not a synonym for *owing to* or *because of* in an adverbial sense.
- **Effect / affect.** *Effect* as a noun is a result; as a verb, to bring about. *Affect* means to influence.
- **Etc.** Not used of persons; avoid after *such as* or *for example*.
- **Fact.** Use only of matters capable of verification, not of judgments.
- **Factor.** A hackneyed word; the constructions using it can usually be replaced by something more direct.
- **Feature.** Another hackneyed word.
- **Fix.** Colloquial for *arrange, prepare, mend*.
- **He is a man who.** Redundant.
- **However.** In the sense *nevertheless*, not to come first in its sentence or clause.
- **Interesting.** An unconvincing word; avoid it as a means of introduction. Make the thing interesting instead.
- **Kind of / sort of.** Not to be used as a substitute for *rather* or *something like*.
- **Less.** Should not be misused for *fewer*. *Less* refers to quantity; *fewer* to number.
- **Line, along these lines.** Hackneyed; cut.
- **Literal, literally.** Often incorrectly used in support of exaggeration.
- **Most.** Not to be used for *almost*.
- **Nature.** Often simply redundant, like *character*.
- **Near by.** Adverbial phrase; not an adjective.
- **One of the most.** Avoid beginning essays or paragraphs with this formula.
- **People.** *People* is a political term; not to be confused with *the public*. Of large groups, *persons* for exact number.
- **Phase.** Means a stage of transition; not a synonym for *aspect* or *topic*.
- **Possess.** Not to be used as a mere substitute for *have* or *own*.
- **Respective, respectively.** Usually omissible.
- **So.** Avoid as an intensifier (*so good*).
- **State.** Not a mere substitute for *say, remark*; restrict to *express fully or clearly*.
- **Student body.** Needless; *the students*.
- **System.** Frequently used without need.
- **Thanking you in advance.** Write *I shall be obliged*.
- **They.** Strunk's 1918 rule objected to singular *they*; **modern usage and this style guide accept singular *they*.**
- **Very.** Use sparingly. Where emphasis is needed, use words strong in themselves.
- **Viewpoint.** Write *point of view*, but not as a substitute for *view* or *opinion*.
- **While.** Avoid as a loose substitute for *and* or *but*; best restricted to its literal sense, *during the time that*.
- **Whom.** Often incorrectly used for *who* before *he said* or similar expressions.
- **Worth while.** Overworked as a term of vague approval.
- **Would.** A conditional statement in the first person requires *should*, not *would* (a 1918 rule; treat as archaic).
